The Great Filter Is Us: AI, Capitalism, and the Coordination Problem

philosophy AI geopolitics

Written in English

The Fermi Paradox asks why, in a universe of billions of stars and billions of years, we haven’t detected signs of other intelligent civilizations. One popular answer is the Great Filter — some barrier so difficult that almost no civilization gets past it. Most discussions place this filter in the past (the emergence of complex life) or in the future (nuclear war, climate collapse). But what if the filter is neither technological nor environmental? What if it’s organizational?

The Coordination Problem

[PLACEHOLDER] Consider the challenge of AI development. Every major power and corporation recognizes the risks of unaligned artificial general intelligence. Papers are written, conferences are held, guidelines are proposed. And yet the race continues to accelerate, because the incentive structure makes slowing down individually irrational. This is the coordination problem at civilizational scale — a prisoner’s dilemma where the stakes are existential.

Market Logic vs. Species Logic

[PLACEHOLDER] Capitalism is extraordinarily good at optimizing for local maxima. It can produce smartphones and streaming services with remarkable efficiency. But it struggles with problems that require global coordination, long time horizons, and the internalization of externalities. Climate change is the obvious example, but AI governance may prove to be the more acute test case, because the timeline is compressed and the feedback loops are less visible.

What Translation Teaches Us

[PLACEHOLDER] Having worked as a translator between languages and cultures, I’ve seen firsthand how coordination failures often stem not from disagreement about goals, but from incompatible frameworks for describing reality. The same dynamic plays out in AI governance: computer scientists, ethicists, politicians, and business leaders are all nominally working on the same problem, but their mental models are so different that genuine coordination remains elusive. Perhaps what we need is not more intelligence, artificial or otherwise, but better translation between the systems of thought that already exist.
